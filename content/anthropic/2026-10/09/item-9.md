Astrophysicist Brice Ménard, a Johns Hopkins University researcher who also works at Anthropic, describes using Claude Science to produce the first complete map of the sky in ultraviolet &#40;UV&#41; light. He says the map will be a valuable educational tool, letting students see the rich structure of the Milky Way at this wavelength. About a third of the map, including much of the galactic plane, was predicted with Claude Science; additional layers label each pixel as "measured" or "predicted" and give uncertainty estimates.

Ménard explains that each wavelength reveals different features: visible light shows mainly stars, infrared reveals dust grains along the Milky Way, radio waves expose hydrogen gas that forms new stars, and X-rays show violent phenomena such as exploding stars or matter spiraling into black holes. For UV light, which reveals dust illuminated by starlight, he previously had to apologize because the only available UV map was full of holes.

No complete UV map existed for two reasons. UV light is absorbed by the atmosphere's ozone layer, so observations must be made from space, and while space telescopes have published UV data for parts of the sky over the past 50 years, much remains unobserved. The largest dataset comes from NASA's GALEX mission &#40;2003–2013&#41;, which imaged about two-thirds of the sky in roughly 38,000 separate observations, but deliberately skipped very bright stars, including those on the plane of the Milky Way, to avoid damaging its detectors. NASA's Swift and South Korea's FIMS/SPEAR added data, yet their combined coverage still has missing pieces. Statistical techniques can estimate the gaps, but doing it properly takes weeks of painstaking, pixel-level calibration and repeated analysis, so such lower-priority projects are often set aside.

This summer, Ménard set out to build the complete map, instructing Claude to gather every available UV dataset, put them on a common scale, merge them into one map, and fill in every patch no UV telescope has observed.
