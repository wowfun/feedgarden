Anthropic published two reports: one on how AI is changing employment inside the company, and one on the gap between how the US public and Anthropic employees expect AI to affect work and the economy.

The first says staff and US workers do not appear to hold different theories about how AI affects jobs; they have different experiences of AI capabilities, which are closely tied to perceptions of the future of work. At Anthropic, adoption has changed hiring: over the past year the company hired for a smaller share of junior positions, and a smaller share of employees work in technical roles. Agent working hours rose sharply. By March 2026 agents worked more hours than employees; by August there were six times more agents than employees, and productivity increased.

Anthropic reports more code per person, which it calls a notoriously imperfect measure, plus more sales per sales representative and more contracts processed. Following the same employees over time, researchers found output rose as AI tool adoption increased. The bottleneck is increasingly human attention, so employees spend more time building ways to monitor agents' work. Anthropic cautions that an AI lab differs from traditional businesses, but says the findings may still be instructive about broader transformations and future AI-native companies.

The second report compares staff with an August survey of almost 11,000 US adults by Morning Consult, weighted to be representative. Both groups expect rapid capability gains, but staff expect faster progress: 39% of US adults and 91% of staff expect AI to make a Nobel-level discovery by 2030. More than half of staff expect AI to do at least 80% of their current work by 2030, versus 5% of Americans. About two in ten staff have expectations similar to the typical US adult. The groups agree on some points: in each, people who expect AI to do more of their own work anticipate similar unemployment increases, suggesting shared economic models but expectations shaped by personal experience.
