Understanding how the brain builds meaning from real-world language is difficult because semantics depend on context that shifts in natural settings. SCRIBE is a framework built on a neuroscience-informed semantic space: it uses an ensemble of open-source large language models to produce interpretable, word-level semantic representations for arbitrary text.

In a behavioral study with 921 participants, the authors show that human judgments of semantic features vary systematically with context, and that SCRIBE captures these shifts, reaching 75–81% of the estimated human noise ceiling. Using brain activity recorded while participants listened to naturalistic stories, they further show that SCRIBE predicts neural responses in language-selective brain regions and significantly outperforms non-contextual semantic representations.

Variance partitioning indicates that SCRIBE explains unique brain-predictive information beyond syntactic features, suggesting its representations capture aspects of linguistic meaning that the measured syntactic features do not account for.

The authors present SCRIBE as a scalable and interpretable foundation for studying natural language semantics in the brain. The work is by Camila Kolling, Omer Moussa, Emin Çelik and Mariya Toneva.
