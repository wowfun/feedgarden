## Article

Wood Mackenzie's latest global levelized cost of electricity &#40;LCOE&#41; report finds that four-hour battery storage is now cheaper than open-cycle gas turbines in all 43 markets where both technologies were modeled. The firm's principal analyst, Ahmed Jameel Abdullah, called the economic shift "decisive and widening," attributing it to gas turbine shortages and rising fuel volatility pushing peaking costs up while expanding battery manufacturing pushes storage costs down.

The report covers Europe, North America, Latin America, Asia Pacific and the Middle East and Africa. Regional highlights it cites include:

- Middle East and Africa: utility-scale solar already leads at &#36;37/MWh, and four-hour storage is forecast to fall a further 33% to &#36;80/MWh by 2035, displacing gas peaking on cost across every gas market in the region.
- China: remains the global storage cost benchmark, with a benchmark LCOE more than 55% below the rest of the Asia Pacific average of &#36;134/MWh, driven by domestic supply chain integration and manufacturing at scale. Competitive Chinese supply is expected to pull the rest of Asia Pacific down to &#36;92/MWh by 2036, though Japan, Australia and the Philippines are expected to stay at a premium because of import duties, elevated installation costs and domestic manufacturing policies.
- Asia Pacific: solar PV remains the most cost-competitive source, but the highest-cost market in 2026 is more than 200% more expensive than the lowest, a gap forecast to widen to 250% by 2030 even as every market improves. Onshore wind costs range more than six times between cheapest and costliest markets.
- Latin America: solar PV with single-axis tracking remains the lowest-cost generation technology in 2026, with average costs expected to fall 38% by 2060. Onshore wind's regional average LCOE is projected to fall nearly 21% by 2030, from &#36;73/MWh to &#36;58/MWh, as Chinese OEM expansion intensifies pricing competition and larger turbines lift energy yields. Grid-scale storage LCOE is forecast to fall 42% by 2060. Offshore wind is a longer-term opportunity, approaching combined-cycle gas turbine parity only in the early 2040s, though activity in Brazil and Colombia is starting to stimulate local supply chains.

On the broader technology mix, single-axis tracker solar is now the lowest-cost new-build technology in 43 of 48 modeled markets, with onshore wind leading in five. In Saudi Arabia and the UAE, solar LCOE is on track to fall below &#36;20/MWh by 2033. The article begins its Europe section by noting solar PV retains its position as the cheapest power technology there, with fixed-tilt LCOE forecast to fall 22% by 2060.

## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters largely welcomed the analysis but challenged parts of it. One praised the writing as rigorous and dense, noting the underlying regional studies cost &#36;9,990 each, and highlighted the finding that storage is now decisively cheaper than gas peaking. Another &#40;sam-cop-vimes&#41; questioned the arithmetic, asking how a 33% fall on &#36;37/MWh yields &#36;80/MWh, apparently conflating the solar and storage cost lines.

Several disputed the modeling assumptions. Bratmon argued the forecast simply assumes battery costs fall 33% over ten years while gas turbine costs rise, calling it "the number we had to put in to get the conclusion we want." nicoburns pushed back, noting battery costs fell roughly 60-80% over the previous decade and that sodium-ion and scale make further declines reasonable, though they could not comment on gas turbines. jl6 and ahartmetz objected to forecasts running to 2060, arguing 34-year horizons are absurd given recent change and that expecting only a 16% wind cost drop implies implausibly slow progress.

Others raised scope questions: adrianN said the hard problem is bridging a cloudy, windless winter week rather than four hours, where gas still looks cheaper; ZeroGravitas replied that open-cycle turbines serve short peaking roles that batteries now do better and are inefficient for long durations. A newcomer asked why four-hour rather than 8- or 12-hour batteries are the focal comparison.
