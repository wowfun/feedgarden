## Article

The author describes a newer style of software-engineering recruiter email: a first paragraph that explains the recipient's own career back to them, frequently generated by an LLM, ending in something like "that's exactly what we're looking for," followed by a job description for work that has almost nothing to do with the experience just cited. Several examples are quoted, including pitches about multi-tenant isolation for AI model serving, golden-path Terraform service templates, IAM and secrets management in CI/CD, policy-as-code, and document extraction where accuracy decides whether a patient is seen or denied.

The author separates this from older templated emails in two ways. First, the "personalisation" has no connection to the job description: the same mail arrives for a Django fullstack role even though the recipient's history is infrastructure work with no professional Python experience. Second, the text is obviously LLM-written, full of common LLMisms, sometimes in the job description itself. The author argues an LLM could instead read a resume and explain how the experience ties to the role, or conclude the person is a poor fit, and suspects the latter is deliberately avoided. A simple edit pass or proofread could remove the telltale phrasing.

The core complaint is that the overt fakeness of the effort makes the outreach feel more impersonal than a plainly templated shotgun blast, and actively insults the recipient's intelligence when a paragraph claims the prior roles make them "perfect for this job" while describing unrelated work.

## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters largely echoed the frustration and added related schemes. One described an email from a well-known tech figure starting a company and inviting a chat, which actually routed the recipient into a normal hiring pipeline with a hiring manager; they objected to misrepresenting outreach as access to an influential person. A reply noted a worse case: a candidate interviewed by an AI imitating a human on video, disclosed on condition that any candidate use of AI would end the process.

Another said generative AI has destroyed a useful trust signal: a logo, a well-designed site or a recruiter email referencing specific LinkedIn experience used to show someone did more than the minimum, and now that effort must be spent verifying whether it was AI-generated. One shared a practical trick: prefixing a first name on LinkedIn with an emoji, so any message opening with that prefix is spam; they were surprised senders have not trained around it.

Others suggested quizzing recruiters with image CAPTCHAs about DevOps pipelines, joking that passing would prove it is a bot, and observed that "reply stop" promises are routinely followed by another email. One view offered: invest in an actual network, which makes ignoring recruiters easy.
