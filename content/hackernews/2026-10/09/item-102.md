## Article

Pentagon chief Pete Hegseth said the firing-squad execution of former Army Maj. Nidal Malik Hasan will be made available for the public to see, calling it an unprecedented step. Speaking to Real America's Voice on Thursday, Oct. 8, Hegseth said Hasan would receive a "firing squad of soldiers" and that "we'll make sure that people are able to watch it, that it's public," adding that "people need to understand that there are serious consequences for these types of things." He confirmed the execution would be livestreamed by replying "correct" to a CBS News report on X.

Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle previously announced the execution would take place at Fort Hood on Dec. 3, and Reuters reported the Pentagon would release further details. USA TODAY said it could find no record of any government ever livestreaming an execution, and the nonprofit Death Penalty Information Center likewise reported no such record. The center's executive director, Robin Maher, said Hegseth's comments "may be more aspirational than based in reality," noting there is no regulation or precedent for livestreaming an execution or making it widely available. The last documented public execution in the U.S. was in 1936, when about 20,000 people attended the hanging of Rainey Bethea in Owensboro, Kentucky, according to Sonya Lee's 2025 book "American Bloodlines."

Hasan, a U.S. Army major, shot fellow soldiers at Fort Hood, Texas, on Nov. 5, 2009, killing 13 people and injuring 31 others, and was sentenced to death in 2013 after a court martial. Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell announced on Oct. 5 that President Donald Trump approved the firing squad on Hegseth's recommendation, writing "Judgment Day has finally come." John Galligan, a lawyer representing Hasan in civil matters, called the decision "abominable" and "akin to a public lynching." The Associated Press reported Hasan could file a habeas petition in civilian court, potentially delaying the execution, though it is unclear whether he will; during his trial he admitted to being the gunman, called no witnesses, and leaked documents showing he told mental health experts he could "still be a martyr" if executed.

The U.S. military has not carried out an execution since 1961, when Army Pvt. John Bennett was hanged at a Kansas prison. The last military firing-squad execution was on Jan. 31, 1945, when Pvt. Eddie Slovik was executed for desertion, according to The Air Force Times. The announcement follows the botched execution of Christa Pike in Tennessee, who was hospitalized in critical condition after two doses of pentobarbital failed to kill her on Sept. 30, renewing scrutiny of lethal injection.
