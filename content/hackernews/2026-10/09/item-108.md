## Article

The essay argues that programmers' willingness to adopt AI tools is inconsistent with the resistance seen across other creative fields, and that the inconsistency rests on a mistaken premise: that programming is not Art. Writers have struck over AI protections, thousands of artists signed protest letters, creative industry groups have filed enough copyright lawsuits that a dedicated tracking site exists, and YouTubers and musicians are frequently hostile. Many experienced programmers, by contrast, accept AI for programming while acknowledging it makes them unhappy and is harming the industry.

The justification offered is that programming is functional and therefore not Art. The author rejects this using John Berger's "Ways of Seeing" and its concept of "mystification": Berger critiques florid art-historical prose describing a commissioned group portrait in ecstatic terms — "subtle modulations of the deep, glowing blacks", "harmonious fusion" — before revealing it was simply work a poor old painter was paid to do for officials who wanted an official portrait. The painting was well executed and suited a mundane purpose; it was not a divine relic. The essay says we culturally mystify painting, sculpture, film and music while ignoring their functional roles in providing decoration, amusement and distraction.

The author extends the argument across writing: we mystify novelists but denigrate journalists, yet functional journalism matters no less, and copywriters receive even less respect. There is no transcendental distinction between novelist and copywriter — many skills overlap and the boundary is an accident of commerce and opportunity, with many famous writers having done both. Working professionally with mundane words is good practice for artistic ones, because mundane creativity is still artistic. The piece closes from the author's own history as a self-described teenage "code poet" who was mocked for it, arguing code can be beautiful.

## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters split sharply. One argues AI is here to stay and that time is better spent on newly opened opportunities than lamenting the shift, likening it to trading horses for cars. A 30-year veteran says he enjoys programming more than ever because the drudgery is gone, and wishes never to hand-write another line — though he notes the job always followed him home mentally; another replies that this "leaving it at work" feeling is a genuine relief, since code no longer feels personally carried into production. A dissenting thread claims most programmers never valued originality and eagerly copy, with a reply warning that clever original code often becomes a maintenance nightmare for others. One commenter, aesthetically inclined, describes beauty in type-level guarantees and cutting 100 lines to 10, but another counters that code is pretty yet not art. A skeptical commenter calls natural-language coding fast to 90 percent and then painful for the last 10 percent, predicting one-off personal apps will soon stop being interesting because everyone can generate them.
