## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters mostly debated the proposal's consequences for US talent pipelines. One argued that when academic brain drain reaches a tipping point, talent will leave like a siphon rather than a trickle, and that countries preparing to attract it will reap a windfall the US spent a century cultivating; a reply pushed back that blaming the government alone is a cop-out, since the administration reflects a politically significant share of the electorate and an election is coming.

A commenter in Scandinavia said the most ambitious, entrepreneurship-minded students go to US schools precisely because they sit close to the startup scene, and that a &#36;100k fee would make those graduates unattractive to startups. Another pointed to other countries competing for talent — the Netherlands' Tulip Fund and South Korea's expanded visas for AI and tech talent — and said it is the first time in decades that top talent is not converging on the US; a reply countered that the top tier will still be in demand at US STEM employers and that English-language programmes remain a draw, asking whether the Netherlands or South Korea teach entirely in English.

On process, one commenter recalled that similar attempts kept being enjoined because the charge amounts to a tax rather than a reasonable processing fee. Another said OPT has been managed through executive action rather than Congress, leaving the overseeing department understaffed and underfunded, with visa work spanning DHS, DOL and DOS. A final commenter summed the proposal up as speedrunning brain drain.
