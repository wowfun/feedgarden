## Article

In 2024 U.S. farmers harvested 14.9 billion bushels of corn at an average yield of 179.3 bushels per acre. Roughly 5.5 billion bushels, about 37 percent of the crop, went to produce fuel ethanol, and the United States remains the world's largest ethanol producer, well ahead of Brazil.

The article works through what that land represents in food energy. At 179.3 bushels per acre and 56 pounds per bushel, an acre yields about 10,000 pounds of grain; at a rough 1,500 calories per pound, that is roughly 15 million calories per acre. Dividing the 5.5 billion bushels by the 2024 national yield gives an acreage equivalent of about 30.7 million acres, not 45 million. On paper those acres represent about 460 trillion calories, or the annual needs of roughly 570 million people at 2,200 calories per day. The author stresses this is not a literal count of people who could be fed: most U.S. corn is field corn rather than sweet corn, diets require far more than calories, distribution, affordability, storage and politics decide who eats, and ethanol plants return part of the grain to feed as distillers grains and other coproducts.

On policy history, U.S. gasoline prices rose sharply in 2005 and 2006. Congress established the Renewable Fuel Standard in the Energy Policy Act of 2005 and greatly expanded it through the Energy Independence and Security Act of 2007; the RFS remains the world's largest biofuel mandate. Between 2006 and 2007 planted corn acreage rose from 78.3 million to 93.5 million acres, one of the largest year-to-year increases on record. Meanwhile other renewables scaled: U.S. wind generation rose from roughly 34 terawatt-hours in 2007 to more than 425 terawatt-hours in 2023.

Whether corn ethanol yields substantially more energy than it consumes has been debated for decades. An influential early analysis concluded that producing ethanol from corn required more fossil energy than the fuel contained; later Department of Energy analyses, incorporating coproduct credits and improvements in farming and refining, found a positive energy balance. Results depend heavily on assumptions about fertilizer, farm energy, refinery efficiency, coproducts and land-use change, and the climate balance is described as equally contested.
