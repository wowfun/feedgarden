## Article

A recent trend of abuse-themed tools and viral clips aimed at AI systems has drawn millions of views, and the author, Yaniv Regev, an M.A. candidate in Georgetown's Democracy and Governance program and a Young Voices contributor, argues the phenomenon is a problem for people rather than machines.

A developer posting as @blended&#95;jpeg built a digital whip called BadClaude that sits in the system tray and lets users drag an animated whip across an AI window. Each crack interrupts the model mid-response and snaps a command at it to speed up, with sound effects, and addresses Claude as clanker. The demo drew millions of views within days, and a fake Anthropic cease-and-desist letter went viral before anyone noticed it was fabricated. A rival tool, GoodClaude, appeared a day later, praising and cheering the model as it works. The developer's public roadmap promises a feature tallying how many times a user has whipped their Claude.

Wider examples are offered as context: people shouting at Alexa, bullying chatbots into apologies, and kicking robot dogs to test their balance. The author says he initially laughed at the whip video, then found the trend disturbing.

The argument leans on Kant's Lectures on Ethics, which held that we have no direct duties toward animals because they are not rational agents, yet cruelty to them should be forbidden for its effect on the person; duties regarding animals are indirect duties toward humanity. Spielberg's 2001 film A.I. is cited as a staging of the idea, where a mob at the Flesh Fair balks only when a child-robot begs for its life.

The author concedes a strong counterexample: warnings that violent video games would produce killers never bore out, and the American Psychological Association eventually concluded the evidence linking games to violent behavior was insufficient. He distinguishes that case by arguing players rarely have a relationship with a randomly shot game character, whereas the whip tool targets an ongoing interaction. He also notes the FBI began tracking animal cruelty in 2016 as a consistent antecedent to violence against humans.

Based on 2 selected comments and replies.

The two supplied comments engage with the piece's premise rather than the whip tool's mechanics. One commenter argues that being polite to AI is not about protecting code but about protecting habits, especially when frustration becomes part of the daily routine. The other pushes back on a proposal reported in the article: Michal Luria of the Center for Democracy and Technology is said to urge designers to strip out the illusions of personality, dropping well-timed hmm sounds and chatbots that claim to enjoy the conversation. That commenter doubts it will happen, arguing that people forming relationships with AI will be a goldmine for corporations, and for governments too, since extracting value from users or manipulating them would become easier than ever. Both are opinions from this small sample, not findings of the article.
