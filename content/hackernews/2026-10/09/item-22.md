## Article

A writer who runs a golf blog says recent attention from a niche "nerd-golf" community has brought a small but steady stream of flattering emails and messages. His routine now unfolds in the same way: a note looks pleasant, something feels slightly off, he runs it through the AI detector Pangram, and receives a 100% AI-predicted result. He then replies to say that Pangram does not care for the sender's writing style. Several senders have admitted it, responding along the lines of "you got me, I use AI to help me write."

The author says the exchange leaves him unable to know whether he is talking to a person at all. His request to readers is blunt: if you are writing because you like his work, do not use AI to compose these short messages. He argues that a quick note takes two minutes and that typos or awkward phrasing are fine, whereas AI-written text removes any signal that a human actually read the blog.

He also describes a practical dilemma. There is a real chance he will report such accounts as spam, which he does not want to do if the sender is a genuine fan. He frames the behavior as poisoning the well, saying it is strange to receive a flattering note and end up unsure whether anyone read his writing in the first place, and that many people do not appear to grasp the wider cost.

## HN discussion

Based on 9 selected comments and replies.

Commenters largely agreed there is a problem but split on what it means and what to do. al&#95;borland called it a broader trend: saving one user a little time wastes the time of many others, and the erosion of social trust compounds the damage; consumer-facing agents will accelerate it. The author replied that many people simply do not consider second-order effects, adding that he is not an LLM hater but blames the internet's global interconnectedness for killing etiquette.

mattm noted that a human was in fact at the other end, so this differs from pure spam, and suggested offering a 15-minute video call to prove humanness and talk about the shared interest; he doubts complaining will change anything. The author answered that he cannot tell spam from engagement farming, citing one message proposing to use his nonexistent "podcast" as an advertisement and another asking leading questions about how his product works.

Others offered different angles. rdmuser said scam bots use the same "you got me" response. segmondy proposed automating replies to AI-detected mail. jventura, a non-native English speaker, argued that someone who uses an LLM to polish words is still a human, though he granted a short message differs from a blog post.
