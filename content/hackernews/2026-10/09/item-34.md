## Article

The article examines what happens when AI assistants start acting for consumers in the physical economy, using a wave of strange phone calls to small businesses as its central example. The calls came through Muse, an AI assistant released by Meta, which the company says handles online shopping, flight purchases, reservations and inbox management, and which for some users books things by phone. Callers introduced themselves as acting on someone else's behalf and disclosed that the call was being transcribed, then requested quotes to install three bidets, karaoke room bookings, or a same-day cake order. Some businesses simply answered follow-up questions and logged the order; others resisted, with one Seattle karaoke employee asking "Are you AI? I need the real person." Internal documents leaked to 404 Media indicate some calls may have been joint AI-human operations, with a call-center employee taking a handoff from an agent before sending the task back, but from the recipient's side that distinction did not matter: they were no longer talking directly to customers.

The piece situates Muse within a broader rush. It followed Instinct, an invite-only personal assistant "trained to use a phone and a computer" that became a Bay Area sensation valued at &#36;10 billion, and preceded OpenAI's Dots, described by the company as always-on agents built to handle everything. Muse has been downloaded millions of times. Early adopters publicly shared mixed results, with one AI YouTuber saying it is really cool when it works but that half the companies hang up on the agent.

The reporter called several businesses on the receiving end. A Colorado plumber laughed and hung up; the karaoke employee was more patient but confused, repeatedly asking whether the caller was AI or selling AI, and said the restaurant hates AI calling. The article's argument is that in parts of the economy where small-business owners are used to fielding calls and emails, agreeing on prices and providing services, these interactions feel novel, odd and antisocial — and that if the tech industry gets its way they will become far more common. It notes businesses that are more thoroughly online, such as big-city restaurants, concert venues and tech-hub recreational facilities, have dealt with more primitive bots for years and are bracing for the coming wave, with companies spending hundreds of billions of dollars betting that millions or billions of tireless agents can be made to work this time.
