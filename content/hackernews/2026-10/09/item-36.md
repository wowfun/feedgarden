## Article

Planet Nine is a hypothetical planet in the outer Solar System, proposed in 2014, whose gravitational influence could explain the unusual orbital clustering of extreme trans-Neptunian objects &#40;ETNOs&#41;. These are bodies beyond Neptune orbiting the Sun at average distances above 250 astronomical units &#40;AU&#41;. The ETNOs tend to reach their closest approach to the Sun in one sector and to have similarly tilted orbits, which some astronomers argue suggests an undiscovered planet is shepherding them.

Early estimates gave the planet a mass five to ten times that of Earth, on an elongated orbit of 400–800 AU. The orbit estimate was refined in 2021 to a semi-major axis of 380 &#40;+140/−80&#41; AU, then to 460 &#40;+160/−100&#41; AU, and to 290±30 AU in 2025. Current cited parameters include an aphelion of 370±30 AU, a perihelion of 200±50 AU, an eccentricity of 0.29±0.13, an orbital period of about 4,940 &#40;+790/−750&#41; years, an inclination of 6.8°±5.0°, a mass of 4.4±1.1 Earth masses, and an apparent magnitude of roughly 21. Konstantin Batygin and Michael Brown have suggested the planet could be the core of a giant planet ejected by Jupiter during the Solar System's formation; other ideas include capture from another star, an origin as a rogue planet, or formation on a distant orbit later pulled eccentric by a passing star.

Sky surveys including the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer &#40;WISE&#41; and Pan-STARRS did not detect Planet Nine, though they have not ruled out a Neptune-diameter object in the outer Solar System, since detection depended on the object's location and characteristics. Further searches used NEOWISE and are ongoing with the 8-meter Subaru Telescope.

The claim remains contested. Some astronomers attribute the apparent clustering to observational bias, given the difficulty of discovering and tracking these objects for much of the year. Discoveries such as 2017 OF201 and 2023 KQ14 have challenged the hypothesis, because their orbits are not aligned with the other TNOs and would be unstable if Planet Nine existed. However, versions of the hypothesis exist that do not require the questioned orbital clustering. Unless the planet is directly observed, its existence remains purely conjectural. The article also notes the historical precedent of Percival Lowell's 1906 search for a trans-Neptunian "Planet X", a name previously used for a different proposal.
