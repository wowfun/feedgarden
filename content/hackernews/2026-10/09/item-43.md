## Article

Large US law firms are hiring fewer entry-level lawyers while lateral hiring grows, and the shift is widely attributed to generative AI taking over the work juniors traditionally learned from. Gretta Rusanow, head of advisory services at Citi's law firm group, says most large firms will have smaller first- and second-year associate populations and that uncertainty grows the further out firms plan.

The numbers

- Firms with more than 500 lawyers hired about 7.5% fewer Class of 2025 graduates, roughly 540 fewer, the first fall since 2014.
- Across the Am Law 200, entry-level hiring has held near 7,400 a year for four years, while lateral hiring rose 12% in 2025 and laterals became 51% of associate hires. In a separate sample of 141 firms, lateral hires outnumbered entry-level hires for the first time.
- Supply moved the other way: law school applicants rose 27% in two years, graduates 8% and first-year students 7.9%.

What firms plan

In a Citi survey of 57 large firms, two-thirds expect fewer first-years in 2028 than in 2025, nearly 60% expect fewer second-years and 19% do not know. An earlier Citi and Hildebrandt survey found 86% of large firms planned to grow associate ranks through 2027, but only 35% planned bigger first-year classes, a shift toward "a more senior demographic ... likely driven by the adoption of generative AI." Few firms are firing juniors; one lawyer describes firms hoping productivity gains mean more work at the same staffing level, with experienced lawyers already reviewing the increased output of AI-assisted juniors. Demand for laterals with AI expertise rose 106% in a year. Retention is weak too: 83% of associates who left firms in 2025 did so within five years, a record.

Where juniors learned

First-pass review, research, diligence and first drafts are the tasks AI does first, and they are where juniors learned. Harvard's Center on the Legal Profession calls this a coordination failure: each firm automating is rational, but collectively they erode a pipeline every firm depends on. One lawyer argues drafting is itself a learning exercise, better than reviewing something "80% right," and notes that learning has long been billed to clients. Seniors report juniors sending AI output unread; one firm fired such a junior after about six months. Ropes &amp; Gray lets first-years devote about 400 billable hours a year to learning AI. 96% of summer associates used AI in 2026, up from 80%, and fewer than one in five worried about their jobs. 76% of practising lawyers expect new graduates to cite-check AI output, but only 20% of third-year students say they are proficient. 71% of firm leaders expect junior roles to change, and 74% say partners are least prepared to use AI. In Los Angeles, six judges and their research attorneys are piloting an "AI law clerk."
