## Article

A University of Vienna article by Laura Schmitt, dated 8 October 2026, interviews mathematicians Nathanaël Berestycki and Vera Fischer about how rapidly AI is changing their field. In September 2026 OpenAI announced that some 10,000 AI agents had partially "solved" the Navier–Stokes problem, one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a USD 1 million prize. A month later, OpenAI released over 700 manuscripts claiming new results across roughly 370 families of problems. The article notes that news of long-standing open problems falling to AI has arrived quickly since May 2026, when an OpenAI representative said its AI had disproved an 80-year-old conjecture by Paul Erdős.

Berestycki, professor of stochastics at the University of Vienna, compares the moment to a tipping point or phase transition, where previously slow growth becomes explosive, like a rumour spreading through a social network. He calls it a major revolution in how mathematics will be done, while also describing the batch-solving of beloved problems as "a bit gut wrenching". Fischer stresses that there is a huge misunderstanding in treating mathematics as merely problem-solving, since the discipline goes far beyond producing proofs at speed.

The Navier–Stokes claim is contested. According to the article, OpenAI denies allegations that it concentrated resources on the problem after learning that Tristan Buckmaster &#40;NYU&#41; and Levent Alpöge &#40;Anthropic&#41; were close to a breakthrough; the reported token spending was equivalent to over USD 22 million. Three days after the announcement, 25 Fields Medallists published an open letter, "A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics", arguing that using mathematics to test AI systems is detrimental to the science of mathematics and citing a mismatch between commercial AI goals and research. The next day, the Association for Human Mathematics launched to protect mathematics as a human endeavour; it drew over 700 members in under three weeks.
