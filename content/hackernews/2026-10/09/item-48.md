## Article

In this 2015 essay, the author reflects on having misread the purpose of everyday conversation. He had read that the point of meeting someone over a beer, coffee, lunch or dinner is that the food and drink spare people from needing to have something to say throughout the whole exchange. He had assumed lunch was mainly about lunch, and that his dislike of conversation came from being an introvert. The reframing suggested conversation may be harder for everyone than he had assumed.

A conversation with his daughter, then a college freshman, forced the lesson. She texted that she wanted to talk; when he asked what about, she was annoyed. She asked if they could just talk about something stupid. On the call they spent a good long while on Donald Trump, the presidential race and similar topics, and only after roughly half an hour did the conversation turn organically to what she actually wanted to discuss. He realised some people need a warmup before they feel ready to raise something uncomfortable. He describes himself as a get-to-the-point person who says what he means or nothing at all.

The essay attributes the difficulty to the mechanics of speech itself. Language is imprecise, feelings do not translate directly into words, and the rationalisations people offer for their wants and emotions are often logically incoherent, while their limited perspective hides that incoherence. Because people intuit this, and because there can be social penalties for saying the wrong thing in the wrong way to the wrong person, speaking about sensitive matters carries vulnerability. Rituals such as idle chit-chat, coffee and shared meals exist to build enough trust to overcome that vulnerability. The author argues this is the great flaw with Twitter: a 140-character format pushes users straight to the point, stripping out the rituals that let people dance around sensitive issues, so the problems those rituals prevent come flooding in. He notes that he is near 50 and suspects others understand this intuitively rather than explicitly.

## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters largely accept the essay's premise but reframe or extend it. paimapi argues the more helpful frame is emotional maturity rather than rhetoric: you rarely know another person's current emotional state, what is nagging at them, or where their ethics and ideologies lie, and those shift over time. Replying to that, veunes notes that being trustworthy and being perceived as trustworthy are not the same thing, and that people who dislike small talk forget the other person has not yet had a chance to establish the former.

nine&#95;k offers a modem analogy: small talk sends signals to assess how the line behaves and what the other side supports, so its purpose is a formal, predictable, zero-surprise exchange, and skipping it can botch communication. asdfman123 likens it to letting a cat sniff your hand, describing a subconscious process of determining someone's values before offending them, which also builds baseline trust.

Other commenters dispute the diagnosis. tyg13 argues the online problem is not a lack of unstructured discussion but the absence of community: there is no rapport or continuity with strangers, and years of commenting produced no lasting relationships. dale&#95;glass counters that it worked for him when he gravitated to places with regulars, such as IRC channels, Discord or forums, rather than HN. ChrisMarshallNY also appears in the thread on the value of ritual and sharing meals.
