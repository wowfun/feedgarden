## Article

In a Current Affairs essay, the author uses Richard Scarry's children's book Busy, Busy Town as a standard for how a city should work. In Busytown, work is legible: the mechanic fixes the motor when something is wrong, the painter paints the walls, city workers fill a pothole. Life is straightforward and things are what they seem. The author contrasts that with the 21st-century US economy, which he argues is hard for ordinary people to survive in partly because so many things are more complicated than they ought to be.

His examples center on healthcare. He has written before about how maddening it is to comparison shop for insurance plans on Healthcare.gov, citing a New York Times-quoted economist who found the system so complicated they gave up comparing plans. Sponsored search results, he says, have led people into insurance scams. Using insurance is a separate ordeal, and he argues that anger over complicated medical billing during a loved one's fatal illness helps explain why so many cheered when Luigi Mangione shot the CEO of UnitedHealthcare. He notes Mangione's confession said that posing as a rich investor to learn the CEO's whereabouts produced the fastest response he had ever received from an insurance company.

Against that, the essay sets Busytown's medical center, where Nurse Nelly, Dr. Lion, Dr. Rabbit and Dr. Dentist simply take care of Lowly the worm and he leaves. The author says Current Affairs has long argued such a system is achievable, and quotes Medicare For All proponent Dr. Abdul El-Sayed saying free-at-point-of-use universal healthcare is not only possible but necessary because "it shouldn't be this hard" to be treated for an illness. He also lists what Busytown lacks: memecoins, the gig economy, junk fees, phishing scams, fine print, deepfakes of Bill Gates hawking fake Alzheimer's cures, sponsored search results, phony reviews and dynamic pricing, plus collateralized debt obligations, credit default swaps, Palantir-style data profiling and private equity buying apartment buildings.

The essay's term, "Richard Scarry socialism," is the author's own coinage, offered as a partial description of what NYC mayor Zohran Mamdani is trying to do. He acknowledges the risk of being seen as forever inventing idiosyncratic "socialisms," having earlier called Mamdani a "suit socialist," and expects Marxist purists to roll their eyes. He frames Mamdani's goal as not merely a fairer city but one that operates the way it should, and calls the Busytown vision an ideal rather than a fantasy. The supplied text is truncated before the argument concludes.

## HN discussion

Based on 1 selected comments and replies.

One commenter questions whether the essay's model is Busytown or Lazytown, while conceding that the author is not assuming or advocating infantilization of the audience. He reads the essay's key point as being about who does what, meaning architecture: children are taught some social architecture, whichever kind and by whatever teachers, since there is no such thing as no architecture at all, and they receive it as an ideal because that is the only category they possess. He adds that calling it an ideal does not mean the needle cannot be moved toward it in real life, and that Scarry's focus on roles and responsibilities may look trivial or naive but is also essential. This is a single comment, so it reflects one reader's framing rather than a range of views.
