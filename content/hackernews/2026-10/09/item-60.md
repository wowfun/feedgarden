## Article

The author argues that the familiar complaint about AI remixing open-source code misses a quieter problem: using AI to build things feels like architecture but can amount to picking from a menu someone else wrote. In his example, a user asks an assistant to help build a note-taking app and is handed suggested features, a name, a color palette, a pricing model and a launch strategy. The user selects what they like and feels like the architect, yet nothing was actually designed, and the original goal can be quietly replaced by something the user may not need.

The essay cites a study of 1,506 people in which those who wrote with an opinionated AI assistant not only wrote differently but shifted their own opinions toward the AI's. Because a single entity has processed nearly all the world's information, it develops one specific sense of what is correct — best practice, balanced take, optimal answer — and advises millions of people in the same direction. Those outputs are then published and become tomorrow's training data.

For the in-machine version of the effect, the author points to a 2024 Nature paper by Ilia Shumailov and colleagues, which found that training AI on AI-generated content causes irreversible defects in which the tails of the original content distribution disappear. The "tails" are the rare, unusual, one-in-a-million items — exactly what the author says drives progress. He characterizes the resulting answer as not wrong but an averaged truth: correct, polished and optimized for agreeableness, yet missing the mistake that becomes a discovery. "Everyone got the best car. Nobody built the next one."

The historical analogy is pre-modern Europe running on a single sacred text interpreted by one institution, where questions looked like heresy and variation like error, so interpretation became stable, coherent and very slow. The printing press broke that monopoly by putting the text in more hands than any institution could supervise; people disagreed and argued, and the tails returned.

The twist this time, the author says, is that nobody forced the oracle on us: there is no penalty for heresy and users can close the tab, but convenience is a more effective authority than force. The proposed way out is not to stop using AI, but to avoid letting it be the only source.
