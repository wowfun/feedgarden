## Article

NHTSA is preparing to update Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 108, the rule governing car headlights, after a long and slow path to the current version. In 2013, responding to a petition from Toyota, the agency said it would begin rulemaking; five years passed before a proposed rule appeared. In the interim NHTSA considered adopting Europe's adaptive driving beam &#40;ADB&#41; rules, but decided against it, reasoning that in some situations — an intersection, or two vehicles oncoming on a curved road — a car with adaptive beam lights "does not have a long preview of another vehicle," leaving ADB too little time to adapt its beam pattern.

A key structural difference shapes the two regimes. In Europe, cars are homologated for type approval: manufacturers submit examples to authorities for testing, and authorities then approve the car for sale. In the US, OEMs self-certify, telling NHTSA their cars comply with the FMVSS and facing sanctions if that turns out to be false. NHTSA judged Europe's glare tests too subjective for a self-certification regime and wrote its own instead. That final rule was ready in 2022, and cars with active beam matrix LED headlights have since gone on sale in the US — Audi's large new SUVs are an example, though limited in functionality compared with the same technology on European-spec cars.

Among NHTSA's stated goals for an updated FMVSS 108: harmonizing US standards with global counterparts; updating rules for LEDs, ambient light sensors, automatic high beams and replaceable light sources; improving test procedures for lenses and optics; better understanding how to aim headlights; and ensuring none of this blinds anyone else on the road with glare.

## HN discussion

Based on 2 selected comments and replies.

One commenter welcomes the rulemaking, describing frequent near-accidents caused by residual blindness from very bright headlights, since lifted pickup trucks and large SUVs aim their lights directly at an ordinary car's rearview mirror. Their proposed fix is a reasonable lumen threshold for manufacturers plus a steep penalty, suggesting 25,000 dollars per violation.

A reply argues new rules would not solve much. Trucks are lifted after purchase, cheap blinding LED bulbs still sell on Amazon, and dealerships are not forced to aim headlights properly after delivery. In this commenter's view, police could already enforce existing headlight regulations to cut a large share of the problem, and blinding oncoming traffic is already illegal.
