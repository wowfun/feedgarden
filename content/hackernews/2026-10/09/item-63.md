## Article

A Frontiers in Psychiatry perspective article argues that circadian rhythm dysfunction is a prevalent, clinically relevant phenotype in a substantial subgroup of people with ADHD, and proposes behavioral circadian interventions as adjuncts to standard care.

The synthesis cites strong associations between ADHD and evening chronotype with phase-delayed biological markers. Sleep problems are described as widespread: insomnia and sleep disturbances affect up to 80% of adults and up to 82% of children with ADHD, delayed sleep-wake timing occurs in up to 78%, and dim-light melatonin onset &#40;DLMO&#41; is delayed by roughly 45 minutes in children and 90 minutes in adults. These changes are said to accompany blunted and delayed cortisol rhythms, reduced pineal volume, and attenuated peripheral clock-gene rhythms &#40;BMAL1/PER2&#41;. The authors note that delays appear even without comorbid mental health conditions, and that objective sleep studies still associate ADHD with sleep-onset latency and efficiency problems after controlling for anxiety and depression.

Intervention evidence is cited optimistically: melatonin and bright light therapy advanced DLMO in both children and adults with ADHD, and emerging data correlate phase advancement with symptom improvement, with winter trials suggesting circadian preference shifts best predict improvement. Sleep programs are said to improve ADHD symptoms, sleep quality, and functioning in children; exercise and multimodal protocols that advance circadian timing in non-ADHD evening chronotypes are flagged as warranting investigation.

The proposed pathway is behavioral-first: routine screening for sleep and circadian disturbances; phenotypic characterization via chronotype assessment, sleep tracking, and DLMO when feasible; fixed wake times, morning bright light, evening light restriction with screen hygiene, and regularized zeitgebers; plus selective low-dose melatonin for confirmed or probable DLMO delays. The authors call for stratified trials to quantify effects on core ADHD outcomes and define responder phenotypes, and explicitly qualify that the phenotype applies to a substantial but not all subgroup.

## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters pushed back on the framing. A self-described chronobiologist with ADHD &#40;50014066&#41; said the associations with ADHD are real, but noted that many brain processes have circadian regulation and could be disrupted by whatever causes ADHD, that causality is bidirectional since ADHD-related behavior changes light exposure, and that ADHD lacks the clock mutations a circadian disorder would imply — so calling it one "feels off." Another commenter &#40;50015706&#41; described finding an unstudied CRY2 clock-gene mutation that was confirmed to delay the clock, and suggested ADHD may be a cluster of etiologies, only some circadian.

On sourcing, one commenter &#40;50013456&#41; warned that Frontiers in is a low-quality outlet many scientists avoid, citing retractions. Others focused on practical experience: one &#40;50013576&#41; proposed that night is quieter, making it easier to think and work without interruptions, and called noise pollution underappreciated. A commenter &#40;50013721&#41; criticized the title as imprecise and irresponsible, arguing ADHD correlates with but is definitionally not a "circadian rhythm disorder," while another &#40;50012315&#41; said the piece is not new, noting its key cited studies date from the 2010s and asking about larger follow-ups. These are individual opinions, not article findings.
