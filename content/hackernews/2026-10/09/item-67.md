## Article

The article describes how rumors of an OpenAI proof of the unique games conjecture pushed three theoreticians to publish their own milestone result on a closely related problem.

On September 11, 2026, MIT professor Dor Minzer began receiving messages asking whether he was close to settling one of theoretical computer science's most famous open questions. The messages alleged that OpenAI — fresh from announcing a proof about fluid behavior — had discovered a proof of the unique games conjecture and might publish at any time. Minzer had not proved the conjecture, but he and graduate students Yumou Fei and Shuo Wang had recently proved a milestone result about a closely related question. Normally their write-up would take months; they rushed it out in three days, posting a 95-page paper with a first-page disclaimer that the manuscript was mathematically complete but not in the shape they wished to share.

The unique games conjecture, first articulated in 2002, concerns computational complexity — the study of the inherent difficulty of problems. Roughly, it says that a problem about satisfying many constraints simultaneously can be extremely hard even when you would settle for a poor approximation of the best solution. A proof would also imply that current methods for many seemingly unrelated problems cannot be improved, moving researchers closer to a unified theory of computational difficulty.

The underlying problems concern satisfying many possibly conflicting constraints at once, as in sudoku or wedding seating — seating friends together while separating guests who do not get along while filling every seat. Exact solutions can be hard or impossible, so researchers often accept approximations, and complexity theorists want to know why approximate solutions are sometimes easy and sometimes hard.

Their decision to publish quickly was vindicated: on October 6, OpenAI announced a proof of the unique games conjecture along with 376 other results across many fields of math. Researchers met that deluge with the familiar mix of alarm and curiosity, but reacted to Minzer's paper less ambivalently — Carnegie Mellon's Ryan O'Donnell called it "another truly great one" from an author with many great papers on the topic.
