## Article

A Current Affairs investigation describes fraudulent video advertising served to elderly and cognitively impaired viewers, using a YouTube ad for "The Pantry Find" as its central example. The ad features a man in a shirt and tie sitting behind a desk with honey and cinnamon sticks, claiming that a "biblical drink" is the cure for Alzheimer's and dementia. The narrator asserts that Alzheimer's is "one hundred percent reversible," that doctors and the pharmaceutical industry conceal the cure out of greed, and that the drink — made from three household ingredients — eliminates memory loss in under 72 hours.

The article checks the claims against known facts. It notes that Israel has roughly 180,000 people living with Alzheimer's, contradicting the ad's assertion that Israel is the only country with no cases. The ad also teases a mystery benefactor named "Bill," described as the founder of the world's largest tech company, whose name cannot be stated because the video was removed more than three times.

Clicking through leads to ThePantryFind.com, a landing page promising a "simple homemade honey ritual." The page lists the company as MARKET AUTO EXPORT LLC, a used car dealership in Miami, which is also the entity Google's ad transparency center lists for the ad. "Watch Now" then opens a fake CNN news page playing an AI deepfake of Denzel Washington holding a jar of honey. The deepfake claims the actor began suffering memory loss in 2025 and had turned down a lead role because he could no longer memorize lines, that he was told he would end up in a wheelchair, and that Bill Gates contacted him after learning of his condition. Gates supposedly told him the Gates Foundation had found neurological diseases like Alzheimer's to be "completely reversible" by clearing "brain rust," and that researchers had identified a "specific medicinal honey" to dissolve it.

The piece also points to signs of fabrication inside the ad itself: the bookcase behind the first speaker contains garbled, apparently AI-generated book titles. The reporting traces the funnel from paid ad to landing page to impersonated news page to deepfake testimonial, illustrating how advertising infrastructure and generative AI tools combine to target vulnerable viewers. The article's assertions about who ultimately operates the scheme remain hedged, since the listed company may not be the real operator.

## HN discussion

Based on 4 selected comments and replies.

Commenters shared experiences and argued over responsibility. One described chasing a scam that targeted elderly homeowners for tens of thousands of dollars and tracing it into Google's own non-public workspace, saying the expected follow-up never came and that their access was cut off instead.

Another commenter, who says they have schizoaffective disorder and are cognitively impaired, called the practice unacceptable and said they could lose their house if scammed. A third recounted an elderly relative who, while downloading an app for a professional test, was convinced by a full-screen ad that his Android phone was missing something and paid hundreds of dollars for a PDF reader subscription; the commenter said it briefly fooled them as well, and criticized Google's ad practices.

A further commenter argued that Section 230 should not apply to ad networks, which should be treated as responsible for the ads they display as if the ads were their own. These are individual viewpoints drawn from a bounded sample, not a consensus.
