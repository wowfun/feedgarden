## Article

US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth said the public will be able to watch the execution of Nidal Hasan by firing squad, and a Defence Department official told the BBC the execution would be livestreamed. Hasan, a former US Army major and psychiatrist from Virginia, admitted fatally shooting 13 unarmed soldiers in a crowded medical building at Fort Hood in 2009. The Pentagon announced earlier in the week that it would carry out the first military execution in more than 60 years, and President Trump approved the defence secretary's recommendation, according to a Pentagon spokesperson.

It is believed the execution, scheduled at Fort Hood in Texas on 3 December, would be the first public execution in the US in almost a century and the first military firing-squad execution since the end of World War Two. John Mills, a lawyer who works with inmates sentenced to death, described the livestream decision as unprecedented in the modern era and on uncertain legal terrain. The UN human rights office told AFP that livestreaming would be tantamount to a public execution and would breach the absolute prohibition of torture. Democratic Senator Chris Murphy called the idea disgusting and reminiscent of Islamic State executions. Vice-President JD Vance cast doubt on the plan, saying he is not sure it will happen and that he will not watch. Trump said Hasan deserved the firing squad but did not mention livestreaming.

Alonzo Lunsford, a former US Army sergeant shot seven times in the attack, supported execution by firing squad as befitting, and said survivors and the families of the fallen should be able to watch uncensored, though he worried about the message to younger people. He plans to attend. The US Conference of Catholic Bishops expressed shock and great sorrow, with Archbishop Paul Coakley calling the decision a barbaric promotion of a culture of death that must be reconsidered. Amnesty International figures cited by the BBC put executions worldwide at more than 2,700 across 17 countries last year, with only two nations carrying them out publicly: 11 known public executions in Iran in 2025 and six in Afghanistan.

Another argued public execution buys implied consent for later killing of state "enemies" and pressures aligned governors to livestream death-row executions as deterrence, pointing to innocence projects and the risk that some condemned prisoners are innocent victims of racism. A reply worried the spectacle will inflame jihadists and feed a cycle of violence.

One commenter challenged Hegseth's stated rationale that people need to understand there are serious consequences, asking what deterrence gap a public broadcast closes for a crime nobody fails to understand, and calling the statement boneheaded. Another simply remarked on the apparently inexhaustible potential for devolution. The sample reflects a strongly critical tone rather than a representative poll.
