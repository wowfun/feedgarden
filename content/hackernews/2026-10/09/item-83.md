## Article

A set theorist who has worked on the Partition Principle explains why he is unhappy with OpenAI's announcement that the principle does not imply the Axiom of Choice, a problem he has publicly offered a bottle of whisky to anyone who solves. He states plainly that he will not be paying up.

The critique is about communication quality, not only about whether the result is correct. For the preprint, he reports that he read the paper but not the Lean code, since he knows little about using Lean and the code was enormous. His assessment: the write-up is unclear and muddled, with a strange structure, terminology that is "a bit off", and theorems stated in ways he would not expect to see proved, naming Lemma 7.4 and Lemma 8.1 as examples.

References are another complaint. One of his own papers on the Bristol model is cited for a basic introduction to symmetric extensions, which he says is not the reference he would have chosen, and at least three unpublished, unrefereed and non-arXiv'd lecture notes are used as sources, including one of his own used for a proposition he is certain appears in published papers. He says a submission of this quality to a journal should receive a desk rejection, comparing the situation to a paper in Swedish being sent to a US journal: the onus is on authors to make their work accessible to readers.

The author stresses that he is not against AI use in general, and that he does use LLMs for consolidating information, making infographics or proofreading emails. His concern is that mathematics lacks a framework for AI contributions, and he points to community questions already in play, such as arXiv rate limiting, AI statements in papers, and whether chat logs should be public or at least available to reviewers and editors. He notes that OpenAI has released a large number of similarly opaque "solutions". Like all comment-thread items, the surrounding discussion is separate from this article's claims.

## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters split on how to respond to the OpenAI preprint and the author's reaction.

- One argues both things can be true: OpenAI's math releases are inconsiderate or harmful, yet they constitute a trove of results available for the taking, and it is a problem when experts on a problem refuse to engage with a breakthrough about it.
- Another goes further, saying this is not merely laziness or low standards but possible scientific misconduct, citing an arXiv paper alleging that a result, including its proof strategy and notation choices, closely matches a preliminary paper OpenAI released on October 6, 2026.
- A counterpoint says the author, as someone passionate about the problem, simply does not want to do the work of understanding the result, while a new generation of mathematicians would happily work through it. A reply notes that junior researchers and postdocs are preoccupied with a poor academic and industry job market and have little patience for this either.
- Two commenters discuss obligation: one compares the situation to being handed a nonsensical AI-written issue analysis that must still be read because a customer shared it; another insists prominent researchers are effectively forced to engage, since the public floods them with messages about the result, though a reply calls that use of "forced" very loose.
- The sample is a ranked selection and shows real disagreement, not consensus.
