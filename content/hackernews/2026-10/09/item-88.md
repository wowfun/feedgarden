## Article

Canadian YouTuber Anthony Sistilli built a do-it-yourself license plate reader designed to watch police vehicles rather than ordinary drivers, a project he says was meant to invert the logic of the commercial Flock surveillance network. After he published two short videos for his 140,000 subscribers, officers from the Peel Regional Police in Ontario visited his home on Sept. 20. Sistilli said the visit lasted about 30 minutes and focused on his intentions and whether he planned to build a map tracking police movements. Officers argued that such mapping could endanger officers and help people committing crimes, and contended that their own cameras and automated license plate reader &#40;ALPR&#41; technology were less concerning than he suggested. Sistilli said his impression was that the officers were unfamiliar with many documented controversies and misuse cases in the United States.

The contrast he highlights is architectural. Flock is believed to operate more than 120,000 devices across 49 states; those cameras photograph every passing plate and add tag numbers and other details to a large database that law enforcement agencies in other cities and states can often query, regardless of where the images were collected. Sistilli's device, by contrast, uses a relatively cheap Amazon camera, his laptop and machine learning software. He says he built the software with artificial intelligence in about 20 minutes. It scans footage in real time for anything resembling a police vehicle and only attempts to read a plate when the software is confident the vehicle belongs to law enforcement. His phone supplies GPS data, and the picture, time, location and plate are stored in a database he can view on a map.

Testing has been modest so far. Sistilli first aimed the camera at images of police and ordinary cars on his computer, then spent an hour standing in an intersection near a police building with a backpack holding a portable power supply, an open laptop and the connected camera. He said the setup detected plenty of ordinary cars but no police vehicles, because he had chosen a headquarters rather than an operational station.

Sistilli told Straight Arrow he never intended to build a giant network mapping every officer and still does not. He described the goal as testing feasibility and using the irony to illustrate what such surveillance means. Attention has far exceeded his expectations: his first clip, uploaded Sept. 4, had more than 1.6 million YouTube views and 523,000 Instagram views by October. The episode, the article argues, illustrates how narrow the middle ground is between agencies that say the cameras help catch criminals and citizens who worry they have created an unprecedented surveillance state.
