## Article

A first-person essay argues that digital enshittification is real but less inescapable than people assume, and that a few deliberate habit changes produce a noticeably better experience. The author concedes that not all of it can be escaped, especially as companies attach AI labels to everything regardless of what users want or need, so the recommendation is incremental: drop only the most annoying Alphabet services, step by step rather than a blanket boycott.

On search, the author has used DuckDuckGo as the default for more than ten years and says early fallbacks to Google eventually stopped; cited advantages include keyboard navigation between results, inline badges for queries such as checking an IP address or unit conversion, easily being set as the default browser engine, less intrusive and less camouflaged advertising, and greater privacy than Google. Brave and Ecosia are named as alternatives the author has not tried, while Brave and Tavily APIs are used within their free tiers for agent search needs.

For email, the author abandoned Gmail after being an early adopter, now paying for ProtonMail, whose free tier is described as ad-free with a shorter @pm.me address; Proton bundles a VPN and a video meeting app called Proton Meet. The piece also begins a section on dropping Chrome, but the supplied text ends mid-sentence, so no details from that part are available.

A commenter's practical migration recipe appears in the discussion rather than the article. The author notes that all alternatives are imperfect and that scale makes replacement hard.

## HN discussion

Based on 8 selected comments and replies.

Commenters broadly agree that switching is worthwhile but describe it as exhausting and a moving target, since acquisitions and shifting priorities keep changing providers. One commenter says they deleted over 100 accounts but still have more than 300, and spent 45 minutes on hold with Sony only to be refused. The author replies with a concrete Gmail exit recipe: register your own domain, have the registrar forward mail to a Proton address or pay Proton about &#36;3 per month to host domain email, which also yields unlimited aliases, then redirect other mailboxes.

Dissent focuses on whether the alternatives are actually better. One commenter objects that DuckDuckGo relies heavily on Bing, which also runs ads, calls the claim of being "way better" unproven and asks for search examples, and criticizes recommending Brave and Ecosia without having tried them. The author answers that results arrive in one shot and are deterministic rather than profile-dependent, unlike Google.

Another thread argues the deeper obstacle is scale: replacing something as large as Reddit or a big library requires huge resources, which invites the same pressures that degraded the original, as Reddit grew from a cheap link list into a costly walled garden. The author agrees scale matters but cites Proton as evidence a less greedy business model can succeed, and says he pays for YouTube Premium despite disliking Alphabet. A commenter quotes Cory Doctorow's "shopping isn't politics," warning consumption choices are inferior to political action; the author pushes back, calling purchases like an EV or home solar political in themselves.
