## Article

Four men detained at the Folkston ICE Processing Center in rural Georgia used the facility's video conferencing software to record a five-minute video describing conditions inside and what they characterize as a hostile takeover of the immigration courts. In the video, the men describe a legal system they see as intentionally dysfunctional, designed to keep people in federal custody for months or years in privately owned facilities that profit from their confinement. The men appear in facility-issued uniforms, their images blurred by the low-resolution cameras on the detention center's tablets.

One of the men, Idris Makhmudov, says he has been detained at Folkston for 27 months. He describes the facility's work program, in which detainees cook, do laundry or clean for what he says is &#36;1 a day — enough, he says, for a 10-minute phone call. Detainees rely on that work income to buy supplementary food at the commissary and to stay in contact with people outside. It is unclear exactly how the video was made; the report suggests the men may have called someone outside who recorded their message.

Samantha Hamilton, an Atlanta-based immigration attorney with Asian Americans Advancing Justice and a member of the Shut Down Folkston campaign, said she received a YouTube link to the video through a Signal chat. She said everyone appearing in the video has since been placed in segregation and is facing retaliation, which she said they expected. Since the video's release, 45 detainees wrote an open letter detailing conditions at Folkston, and an estimated 73 detainees are on hunger strike.

The article notes that ICE detention is not supposed to be punishment but is meant to ensure people appear for deportation proceedings, yet argues the facilities are virtually indistinguishable from prisons. Folkston was a prison before it became an immigrant detention center. Hamilton said each pod of 60 to 70 detainees has access to only three or four tablets, their sole link to the outside world.

Those communications carry fees. Detainees use GettingOut, software owned by the prison communications company Telmate, to send messages, share photos and make calls. Earlier this year Telmate was ordered to pay &#36;4.23 million to settle a lawsuit over a 2020 data breach in which names, payment details and social security numbers were accessed. Telmate is owned by ViaPath Technologies, formerly GlobalTel&#42;Link, which is owned by the private equity firm American Securities. ViaPath charged users 18 cents to &#36;1.10 per minute in 2021, according to data obtained by the Prison Policy Initiative. In 2024 the FCC capped such rates at 11 to 25 cents per minute depending on facility size; in 2025 the FCC voted to raise the cap by up to 83 percent. The article adds that detainees often struggle to recover money left in these systems after release or deportation.
