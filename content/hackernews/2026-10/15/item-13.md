## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters pushed back on the title's framing. Animats argued the bottleneck is separation, not mining, citing a crowded field of startups with shaky claims: one with roughly &#36;1 million in investment and seven employees promising a 2025 pilot plant, and Australia's Northern Minerals still working toward a definitive feasibility study after five years, its shares far below the 2021 peak.

dwd, a self-described shareholder, said Northern Minerals drew an unprecedented Australian Treasury demand that Singapore-based PRC-linked entities divest about 500 million shares, that these were passed to other PRC-linked entities instead, and that two failed board takeovers plus a postponed annual general meeting set the company back roughly a year.

Replies questioned how special rare earths are. One quoted the standard point that the label is a misnomer: the elements are relatively abundant but thinly dispersed as trace impurities, so usable purity requires processing enormous ore volumes at high cost and energy use. Another stressed that holding minerals differs from extracting and separating metals, and that China's processing capacity means much ore mined elsewhere still ships there.

Others debated causes and remedies. One said price signals for energy, labor, pollution tolerance, legal costs and scale simply pushed the work elsewhere, and that warnings this held only if globalization persisted were dismissed as depressing. One blamed roughly four decades of finance-driven economics; a reply cited an analyst's view that lost mining and refining exemplify absent strategic supply-chain planning. Another said a shutdown mine still counts as a loss and asked whether the dispute is over the word "lose." A final suggestion: the government should just start a rare earth company and contract out operations, as with enriched uranium. These are individual opinions from a small sample, not consensus.
