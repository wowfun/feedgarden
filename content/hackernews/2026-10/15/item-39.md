## HN discussion

Based on 8 selected comments and replies.

Commenters welcomed the site but pushed back on its premise. snide, who lives beside a state park, says the biggest practical problem is the shared fence line: upkeep is technically the agency's job, but 200-foot trees on public land come down in storms and take the fence with them, and a dog owner cannot let that slide. fultonn disputes the idea that a government neighbour means nobody maintains anything, arguing that how public land is treated depends as much on the individual land manager as on the jurisdiction. windowshopping reports the listings themselves are unreliable, citing a "50 acre" parcel that resolved to a two-bedroom condo with no land and a "1400 acre" listing that turned out to be 0.14. kyleblarson describes a positive experience on 60 acres bordered on two sides by Washington DNR land, closing his gate only during rifle deer season, and notes his crossing easement and the roughly 35-year renewal norm. Anon1096 asks how often people actually stray across boundaries, suggests stating on the home page why anyone would want this, and says the state pages do not explain how "bordering" differs from "adjacent" or use feet. The author, otherwatersflow, replies that foot traffic depends heavily on the specific land — hunters appear next to state game lands while large national forests may see almost no one — and clarifies that bordering means parcel lines genuinely touch with a measured shared edge, while adjacent means no shared edge but within about 250 feet, usually across a road or a strip of other land.
