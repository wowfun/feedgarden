## Article

The New Yorker profiles Apollo Robbins, a former Las Vegas entertainer known as the gentleman thief, as he consults on Noah Segan's film The Only Living Pickpocket in New York. For John Turturro, playing an old-school pickpocket named Harry lost in a world of cryptocurrency, learning the craft becomes a way into the character. Robbins demonstrates techniques in real time, from reading a mark's bags and pockets to more subtle moves like reefing items up from outside a pocket, while noting how fashion trends such as baggy jeans can create new opportunities.

What emerges is less a how-to than a study of performance and ethics. Pickpocketing, Robbins and Turturro agree, requires finesse and is usually nonviolent, drawing comparisons to acting in its need for misdirection and timing. Robbins' own path blends crime and magic, having learned from older half-brothers before discovering magic at fourteen and deliberately merging the two worlds. The article traces this hybrid craft through demonstrations with a magician and with Turturro himself, showing both its balletic precision and the line between showmanship and theft.

Ultimately, the piece frames pickpocketing as a kind of urban theater rooted in attention and distraction. Rather than glorifying crime, it examines the skill, discipline, and coded language of the trade, while placing it against a changing New York that Robbins' old-school thief would no longer recognize.
