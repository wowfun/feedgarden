## Article

Two Boston Globe journalists reflect on why Captain Jean-Luc Picard, as played by Patrick Stewart, resonated so strongly with audiences. Rather than relying on swagger or cynicism, Stewart rooted the character in emotional truth. His authority came from moral clarity paired with a willingness to show vulnerability, allowing Picard to be both a credible leader and deeply human.

That balance was on display in concrete episodes. When Picard returned to his French village to heal from a traumatic assault, Stewart shifted from total collapse into slow recovery, selling a raw depiction of a broken man learning to grieve and reconcile. In moments like the Enterprise's witch-hunting episode, his conviction in delivering Picard's warning about censored speech gave the speech its force. For many, the most moving example was The Inner Light, in which Picard lived an entire lifetime on another planet and came to see an ordinary life of family and community as just as great an adventure as exploring space.

These qualities offered an alternative to the anti-heroes that would later dominate prestige television: a model of manhood that was patient, principled, and open about emotion. Stewart's Picard functioned as a kind of space dad for generations of viewers. The show had its campy elements, from giant tar pits to outlandish costumes, but Stewart knew how to make even those moments work. The lasting appeal, as the essay argues, lies not in spectacle, but in a hero whose strength was measured as much by humility and empathy as by duty.
