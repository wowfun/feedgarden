## Article

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine report concludes that long-term zinc creep in cable spelter sockets was the root cause of the 2020 collapse of the NSF's 57-year-old Arecibo Observatory telescope in Puerto Rico. The telescope's main platform hung above the 305-metre reflector dish on cables; each cable ended in a socket filled with zinc that held it in place. Over time the zinc lost its hold, allowing several cables to pull out and the platform to fall into the reflector. The failure sequence began with Hurricane Maria, more than three years before the collapse. Before the hurricane, indications of cable pullout were minimal; afterwards, visual inspections showed large and progressive pullouts that, the report says, should have prompted remedial action. Safety-factor calculations made after the first cable failure did not recognise the accelerated time-dependent materials failure process that governed the eventual zinc pullout. Zinc-filled sockets of this kind have been used for more than a century, making this the first documented case of long-term zinc-induced creep failure. One possible explanation the report offers is long-term low-current electroplasticity induced by the telescope's own electromagnetic waves, which may have accelerated the zinc creep. The report recommends that NSF offer the remaining socket and cable sections for study, and says careful monitoring should increase as facilities age, with explicit funding for detailed, ongoing condition maintenance and monitoring. It also contains recommendations on operations and maintenance manuals, inspection regimes and independent audits, and notes that custom-designed research facilities can fail in unprecedented ways that prior designs cannot fully anticipate.
