## Article

Shiny object syndrome &#40;SOS&#41; is a pop-cultural, psychological concept in which people focus on an idea that is new and fashionable, regardless of how valuable or helpful it may ultimately be, and then drop it entirely once something newer can take its place. Although the idea may seem worth attention in the moment, it is ultimately a distraction—either a personal distraction or something done intentionally to distract others. People who experience fear of missing out are especially susceptible, because the distraction of the “shiny object” clouds judgment and focus. The term is also used when someone mistakes a small, focused matter for the whole picture and fixates on it to the point of losing the big picture. It appears across management literature, popular psychological literature, and the social and computer sciences, and is related to the appeal to novelty and novelty bias. Cited sources range from 2011 to 2020: James A. Roberts’s 2011 book “Shiny Objects: Why We Spend Money We Don’t Have in Search of Happiness We Can’t Buy”; a 2015 AADE in Practice article on why new ideas take so long to catch on; a 2017 Leadership &amp; Organization Development Journal article on maximizing the impact of executive assessment and development; a 2017 Entrepreneur piece; a 2015 New York Times article on the politics of distraction; Forbes and Inc.com columns on avoiding the syndrome in marketing and entrepreneurship; and a 2019 Computer Fraud &amp; Security article arguing that headline hype disguises security failings.

Commenters debated whether “shiny object syndrome” is anything more than a rebranding of novelty bias &#40;codeulike&#41;. venning recalled using the phrase and its “SOS” abbreviation as early as 2005 and asked where it originated, noting the Wikipedia entry’s earliest citation is 2011. d4v3 criticized the entry’s wording as imprecise and unserious, while onemoresoop compared the behavior to “magpie developers” chasing trends for their own sake. Several commenters offered analogies: Ozzie&#95;osman called it “Toddler Soccer Syndrome,” and rf15 said they knew it as “dogs-with-ball syndrome” and used that analogy to teach their son not to chase the ball in football. The thread thus mixed skepticism about the term’s novelty and phrasing with recognition that chasing new trends for their own sake is a real pattern.
