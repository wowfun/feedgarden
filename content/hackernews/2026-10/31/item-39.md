## Article

Voyager 1, NASA's farthest spacecraft, unexpectedly went silent for two days in mid-October 2026. The Deep Space Network did not receive its planned check-ins on Oct. 18 or 19, but the probe, about 16 billion miles away in interstellar space, signaled back on Oct. 20 without engineers needing to send repair commands, and no further outages have occurred. The cause remains under investigation. Engineers have found that one set of thrusters shut down, with a backup set taking over automatically. The small thrusters point and steady the spacecraft, and Voyager 1's computers switch to backups whenever they detect a problem with the active set, placing the craft in a safe mode. Mission manager Kareem Badaruddin said this was the first time the spacecraft had done something like this and that the team expects the unexpected as the mission winds down. The outage adds to signs that the 1977 mission is ending. Voyager 1's radioisotope thermoelectric generators lose about four watts per year, prompting engineers to turn off science instruments to conserve power. On Nov. 18 it is expected to reach one “light-day” from Earth, about 16.09 billion miles. It travels at 38,000 mph and, with Voyager 2, has explored four planets and 48 moons; Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in August 2012. In September, engineers extended power by turning off three instruments on each Voyager and replacing them with three lower-powered alternatives in a plan called “the Big Bang.” Because the swaps also help keep the spacecraft warm in freezing space, they had to occur simultaneously to spread heat evenly.
