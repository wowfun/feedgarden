## Article

At the recent EMFCamp, the author bumped into Professor Kate Devlin, who was discussing the AI Resist List; the author argues one useful response is to loudly complain about poor uses of AI. As an example, they reported malformed calendar invites from Ocado, a British online supermarket, through its customer service form, with a detailed technical analysis. The reply, signed “Lulu,” struck them as AI-generated: it opened with sycophantic agreement, used an em-dash, and felt generic, giving no confidence a technically competent human had reviewed the issue. The author then vented in the follow-up satisfaction survey, giving one-star ratings across every metric and complaining about an AI handling a genuine complaint. Days later a second message arrived apologizing, insisting a real person was typing, and thanking them for the detailed diagnostic, saying it had been passed to the team. The author found this reply more human—imperfect grammar, rambly sentences, and actual engagement with the message. The author concedes AI triage, summarizing verbose messages, and aggregating themes can make business sense. But using AI to reply to a human is disrespectful, because it values the customer's time below the organization's. They encourage calling out poor AI responses and insisting a human engages.

## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters disagreed about whether the second, seemingly human reply was actually AI. mixedmath and hyperionultra argued it was likely LLM output guided by a style sheet, making it nearly undetectable; pdpi countered that it read like over-coached PR-speak and questioned why an automated follow-up would be deliberately delayed. jjgreen shared that an AI-generated thank-you from another retailer left them so insulted they would never send praise again. tln defended AI replies as potentially faster and thus more respectful of customers' time, arguing quality matters more than authorship. CapmCrackaWaka warned that accusations of AI authorship have become a new witchcraft, with people unable to reliably distinguish human from machine writing. Several commenters noted Ocado's generally good service, with delivery drivers on lower quotas, as background.
