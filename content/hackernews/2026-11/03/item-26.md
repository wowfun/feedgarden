## Article

In August, U.S. District Judge Robert Shelby granted Utah’s motion for summary judgment and denied Kalshi’s request for a preliminary injunction, concluding that the federal law Kalshi relied on does not preempt Utah’s ability to enforce its anti-gambling laws. Shelby pointed to statutory text stating that CFTC jurisdiction does not supersede or limit the jurisdiction of U.S. or state courts and explicitly provides for state regulation of gaming laws in most cases. Kalshi is appealing the loss to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit but could not obtain an injunction blocking enforcement during the appeal; on September 8, Tenth Circuit judges denied its emergency motion. Utah filed suit seeking to shut down Kalshi about six weeks later. The CFTC later sued Utah using arguments similar to those the district court already rejected, and argued that Utah is not the first state to attempt to invade the Commission’s exclusive jurisdiction over swaps. The CFTC also noted that several federal courts, including the Third Circuit, have barred states from enforcing gambling laws against CFTC-regulated exchanges offering sports-related or other event contracts. The Third Circuit held that New Jersey cannot regulate sports bets on prediction markets, but Kalshi lost comparable cases in the Sixth and Ninth Circuits, where rulings favored Ohio, Tennessee and Nevada. Because the federal appeals courts are divided, the Supreme Court is likely to decide whether states may apply their gambling laws to prediction markets. New Jersey has petitioned the Supreme Court to resolve the circuit split, and 39 other states plus the District of Columbia filed a brief supporting that petition. The article was originally published on October 28 and updated on October 29 with information that felony charges were filed against Kalshi and its co-founders for violating Utah gambling laws.
