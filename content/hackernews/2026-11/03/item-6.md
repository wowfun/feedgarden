## Article

Subvocal is a YC F26 startup building smart glasses that let users silently mouth words—without moving their lips or opening their mouth—and have them decoded to text at speaking speed. The founders, Aarush and Arnav, were CS students at Georgia Tech who began the project at Stanford's TreeHacks in February, initially prototyping with EMG electrodes on the face. EMG proved impractical due to sweat, electrical interference, placement variability, and user discomfort, so they switched to radio frequency &#40;RF&#41; sensing. The glasses emit low-power radio waves similar to Bluetooth that pass through the face and are modulated by the tongue, lips and soft palate during silent mouthing. Antennas are embedded in the frames, positioned so their fields overlap the articulators. Foundation models trained on large volumes of prompted speech data decode the perturbations into text. Cross-user generalization was a major hurdle because facial anatomy varies widely; the team addressed it by massively scaling pretraining data, collecting recordings from paid participants wearing the glasses. The result is a short calibration &#40;about 20 sentences&#41; for new users, as demonstrated with a YC member. The founders describe the work as first-principles engineering across antenna design, electronics and machine learning. They plan further R&amp;D over the coming months to turn the prototype into a product. The device is intended for use in quiet or public settings where speaking aloud is impractical, such as libraries or offices.

## HN discussion

Based on 10 selected comments and replies.

Commenters reacted with enthusiasm, calling the project sci-fi-like and noting potential accessibility and hands-free computing benefits. Practical questions dominated: one commenter asked about fit for non-average nose shapes, whether processing happens on-glasses or via an app, and how a wake word works; another asked whether the glasses could attach to existing prescription frames, and the founders replied that integrated frames are needed for consistent antenna placement. A privacy concern asked whether RF sensing could infer sensitive states and whether it works offline; a commenter agreed offline-only operation would be necessary. Others noted the technology could enable dictation in loud environments such as yard work, and compared it to fictional “whispering earring” concepts.
