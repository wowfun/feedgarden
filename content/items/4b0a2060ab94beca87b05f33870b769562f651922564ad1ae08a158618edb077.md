A golf blogger who runs a niche site popular with a small community of technically minded golfers describes a recurring problem: short, flattering emails and messages from readers that turn out to be AI-written. The blog's recent attention has produced a small but steady stream of such notes, and the author says the pattern has become hard to ignore.

In the exchange the author recounts, the first step is suspicion — "this is a nice email. Wait, something seems off." The message is then run through Pangram, an AI-text detection tool, which returns a 100% AI-predicted verdict. When the author replies with a friendly heads-up that "Pangram doesn't really like your writing style," the sender admits: "Ahh, you got me. I use AI to help me write."

The author's concern is not spelling or style but uncertainty about whether any human actually read the blog. Writing a quick message takes about two minutes, and minor typos or awkward phrasing do not matter to the author. What matters is that AI use in these exchanges removes the signal that a real person is on the other end, a practice the author calls poisoning the well. The author also notes the awkward alternative: there is a nontrivial chance an apparently AI-generated fan message gets reported as spam, a step the author is reluctant to take against someone who may genuinely be a reader.

The post describes the outcome as disorienting — receiving a flattering note while being unable to tell whether the sender read the blog at all.
