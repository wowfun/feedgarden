Latent Space host interviews Ian Quigley of Leash Bio, a company specializing in DNA Encoded Libraries &#40;DELs&#41; — a technique for collecting very high-throughput interaction data between proteins and small molecules. The host notes having previously heard "nothing but horror stories" from DEL users, yet credits Leash with taming the technique to extract clean, reproducible data suited to large-scale modeling. He attributes that result to what he calls epistemic rigor and a conviction that you simply have to do the thing.

The conversation covers why DELs are difficult for practitioners, and how Leash addresses a core failure mode: putting dozens of barcodes on each molecule so that proteins binding the DNA itself, rather than the drug, can be caught. Two earlier pieces of Leash work are cited as context: a Kaggle competition whose results showed that protein-ligand binding has not actually been solved, and a finding that knowing a molecule's author may be nearly all you need to predict how good that molecule is.

Near the end of the recording, Quigley previewed something his team was then only experimenting with: an AI playground in which agents perform verifiable scientific reasoning. The data collected over the preceding years could supply tens of thousands of "lab-in-the-loop" simulations, which the host frames as exactly what an agent needs for scientific reinforcement learning.

Two months later that effort has matured into Minos, and the team is releasing a full preprint, a blog post, and an open dataset. The host says a follow-up lightning podcast with an update will be released within a few days. The episode is pitched at both complete chemistry novices and expert AI-for-biology practitioners.
