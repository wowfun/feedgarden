The author argues that people overcomplicate venture investing, and lays out four steps: you have to win the right to invest; find and invest in exceptional founders; do so just before it becomes consensus; and do it consistently every year.

He says the vast majority of venture capitalists get perhaps one of these right and some manage a few, but to be truly great you have to do all of them right. In his view the consistency of being right is very hard and less discussed than the individual steps.

He frames the required mindset through a tennis analogy, citing Djokovic: wanting it, enjoying hitting the ball every day, and always focusing on the next point to be won, regardless of track record, accumulated wealth, past misses, the TVPI of the current or last fund, or which star general partner left the firm, because the next opportunity may be around the corner and may go to someone else if you did not want it enough.

He acknowledges the counterargument that the feedback loop is long and that performance can be faked for a while, especially after an early hit under a power-law distribution, but says this eventually catches up, in how you think, how you meet founders, and your own ability to capture the next wave.
